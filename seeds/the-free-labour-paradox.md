# Nobody wants the job once everything is free

If the things people need reach zero cost, the salary loses its power to
motivate. The jobs that still genuinely need a human then have no one willing to
do them. **Abundance undermines the incentive to produce the residue that
abundance cannot yet reach.**

## In his words

> This is a problem of productivity transition and how you deal with the
> retention of certain products that require human labour BUT those humans don't
> have an incentive to do the job because they don't need the salary as all they
> need reached FREE levels
> from: 2026-09-07-deflation-metric-and-labour-paradox

## Stories and evidence

**The two ideas collide, and the collision is the interesting part.** He
proposes the zero-cost count in one paragraph and this in the next. Baumol's
cost disease says that when productivity rises in one sector, wages rise across
the economy, so the labour-intensive sectors that cannot automate get relatively
*more* expensive. Health, education and care are the standing evidence: costs
rise while manufactured goods fall.

So his own two claims predict opposite motions in the same economy. **The count
of zero-cost goods rises, and the price of the human-required residue rises with
it.** That is not a flaw in the metric. It arguably makes it diagnostic: the
metric shows you where residual scarcity is concentrating.

**He is describing a strong form of a known mechanism.** The reservation wage is
the lowest wage at which someone will accept a job; below it they choose leisure.
The backward-bending labour supply curve is what happens when the income effect
overtakes the substitution effect and people work less as pay rises. His case is
the limit: **if needs are met at zero cost, the reservation wage goes to
infinity for any job whose only offer is money.**

**"Retention" is the tell.** He frames it as keeping people in jobs rather than
filling them, which suggests he pictures this happening to a workforce that
already exists.

## Caveats and edges

He names the problem and does not resolve it. No proposal for who does the
remaining work or why, which is honest given it was two paragraphs.

The classical answer is compensating differentials: unpleasant necessary work
pays more. That answer survives here only if money still buys something the
person wants, which is the assumption his own premise removes.

Non-monetary motivations (status, meaning, craft, obligation, community) are the
obvious other route and he does not mention them.

## Gaps

- **Does he think there is a way out, or is this a genuine dead end?** The
  strongest version of the piece needs him to say.
- Which products does he have in mind that "require human labour"? A concrete
  list would sharpen this enormously. Care work? Trades? Judgement roles?
- Has he noticed the tension with his own deflation metric? If yes, what does he
  conclude from it? That question is probably a whole piece on its own.
