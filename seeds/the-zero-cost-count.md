# Count the things that cost nothing

Abundance has no scoreboard, so the argument never resolves. He proposes two
numbers that businesses could target and countries could track: **the rate at
which product costs are deflating, and the total count of products that cost
close to zero end to end**, production and distribution together.

## In his words

> A possible path we could have businesses target and countries track towards
> abundance is the rate of deflation of products and the total number of
> products that cost close to 0 to produce and reach the consumer (end to end
> production and distribution at zero cost).
> from: 2026-09-07-deflation-metric-and-labour-paradox

## Stories and evidence

**Why this is the strongest thing he has proposed.** His first rant asked what
companies should do with their profits. The research answered that arithmetically:
the combined profit of Nvidia and Alphabet spread across humanity is 8.3 cents
per person per day, and closing the $30/day gap costs about 62% of global GDP.
Redistribution is short by a factor of twenty. **The only mechanism that closes a
gap that size is the cost of the thing falling.** This metric measures exactly
that, and he arrived at it without seeing the arithmetic framed that way.

**It converts a slogan into a scoreboard.** "You will be able to X" becomes "how
many X are at zero, and how fast is the count growing". A country can report it.
A company can be held to it. Neither can be done with "abundance".

**End to end is the load-bearing part.** Distribution included. That is what
separates a real zero from a marketing zero: the cost did not move, it moved to
somebody else.

**Ties to demonetisation.** Diamandis has been making the cost-collapse argument
for years (roughly $900,000 of 1969-1989 goods now arriving free with a phone).
Fer's contribution is not noticing the collapse; it is proposing that we
**instrument** it.

## Caveats and edges

He does not say what basket the deflation rate is measured against, and the
choice of basket decides the answer. Nor where "close to 0" is drawn.

The awkward case is goods that are free to the consumer and expensive to
produce, which describes most of the ad-funded internet. The metric needs to say
whether that counts, because if it does the number is already large and
meaningless.

## Gaps

- What basket? Chosen by whom?
- Does free-to-consumer, paid-by-advertiser count as zero cost?
- Is the count of zero-cost goods, or the share of a household budget they
  cover, the better number? The second is harder and probably more honest.
