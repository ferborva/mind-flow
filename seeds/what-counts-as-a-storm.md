# What counts as a storm

Four answers from one short conversation on 10 September, about what the
weather station is actually watching for. **Together they are one provisional
working definition.** Fer's own reading on 2026-09-11: individually
these did not carry enough depth to stand as separate seeds.

## His words

**What a storm is.**

> a storm can be considered as a point of major shift in the social contract that can lead to a large enough disturbance of social fabric.

**How big it has to be.**

> let's say a change that can affect 5% of society in a country or more.

**Where it happens.**

> Each country could/would have their own "storm"/crisis  moments in isolation or there could be a broader one hitting globally or semi-globally at the same time.

**What to watch.**

> Those could be good overall as it's a larger monitoring surface area. However, I would simplify it by access to means to generate income as that is a fundamental key to access many others and be part of the social fabric

Source: [his 10 September answers](../capture/2026-09-10-storms-as-social-contract-shifts.md#his-answer-verbatim)
and [clarification](../capture/2026-09-10-storms-as-social-contract-shifts.md#his-clarification-verbatim).

## What this gives us

A storm is a **social-contract shift**, not an unusual market movement. The
proposed scale is **5% of a country's population or more**. Storms can be
**national or shared**, so country breadth matters alongside the Australian
worked example at depth. And the monitoring focus narrows to **access to means
of generating income**, which he treats as the key that unlocks the others.

He relates income access to security, food, work, education and health in the
preceding captured sentence. The simplification does not claim those needs are
identical.

## What this does not settle

- Whether a *beneficial* shift counts as a storm, or only a harmful one.
- How a social-contract shift is observed at all. A market series is not proof
  of the construct.
- The population denominator, who counts as affected, over what period, and
  whether the effect has to persist. His household-dependants follow-up is
  still unanswered.
- Reading "5% of society" as five percentage points of population is Ren's
  operational gloss, not his settled rule. A relative 5% change in a series is
  a different quantity again.
- How many countries make an event regional or global, the window for
  co-occurrence, and any cross-country aggregation rule. Treating shared storms
  as co-occurring national ones, and never summing affected shares, is Ren's
  operational reading.
- Which indicators show disrupted income access, and how severe counts.
  Unemployment, underutilisation, wages and poverty are Ren's candidates.
  Informal work, hours and job quality need their own evidence.
