# Conditions have owners, and the owner tells you what to do

Not all ifs are the same kind of thing. Some are defended by the organisation
that names them. Some have to be cleared by the technology itself. Some live
between organisations, in contracts, procurement and liability. **Who holds a
condition decides what you can do about it: if it is yours, act; if it is the
technology's, wait and watch; if it belongs to a counterparty, it is a
negotiation and it moves on a completely different clock.**

## In his words

> Some of the conditions [are] indeed closer to the company's immune system, and
> others being limitations that have to be overcome by the technology itself, or
> potentially by some factors external to the organisation, or evolution of
> contractual agreements between enterprises.
> from: 2026-09-08-abundance-frame-conversation

His own worked example, running the method on his day job:

> We will be able to, in Atlassian, unleash the AI to develop the next
> generation of products if the quality of the models is trustworthy enough, and
> our processes and regulations for code verification, SOC 2 compliance, peer
> reviewed green code, etcetera, all our internal processes and compliance
> measures are met by the technology and the technology advances, and our
> process can work within it.
> from: 2026-09-08-abundance-frame-conversation

On conditions versus constraints, and on paying to ignore one:

> Taking down conditions and taking down constraints can be understood in a very
> similar way. Sometimes that's just measured risk. And sometimes you do have to
> take that risk in business in a world to progress anything. That's part of the
> negotiation itself. That soft negotiation, unless you're negotiating with
> physics.
> from: 2026-09-08-abundance-frame-conversation

## Stories and evidence

**The Atlassian sentence has five ifs and every one of them is internal.** Not
physics, not price, not law: process, compliance, peer review, model trust. Put
to him in Salim Ismail's voice: that is what an organisational immune response
sounds like from the inside. Nobody says "we reject this because it threatens
us", they say "our verification standards are not met yet". The ExO answer is
that you cannot work that list down from inside, because it is defended by the
people who would have to remove it, so you stand up a parallel organisation and
let it demonstrate the conditions were never load-bearing.

**His reply is the third category, and it is the better cut.** A parallel org
tests the immune-system conditions brilliantly and reveals nothing about the
ones held by somebody else. Stand up the shadow team, unleash everything, and
you still cannot ship to an enterprise customer whose procurement demands SOC 2
attestation, or accept liability your insurer will not cover. Those conditions
are held by another organisation's immune system, and yours has no jurisdiction
over them.

**Musk's sort, and where it fails.** Put to him in Musk's voice: every condition
is guilty until proven physics, and enumeration is how nothing happens. Delete
the ones that are somebody's preference and absorb the consequence. He does not
accept the "that takes capital and appetite for risk almost nobody has"
objection, and calls it measured risk instead of privilege, which he thinks is
ordinary business. What survives from the exchange is a column on the list: what
does ignoring this condition cost, and can you pay it.

**Condition or constraint is the same activity, and a different posture.** He is
right that taking them down is one job. The word still does work: a constraint
is a wall you push on, a condition is a state of the world that has to be true.
The first addresses people who can push. The second addresses everyone else,
which is most people, and it is why the frame reads as a lens for observers
rather than a to-do list for founders. *(His point; the distinction is
Claude's, and he half-conceded it.)*

## Caveats and edges

**Naming a condition legitimises it.** Some ifs are real and some are hostages,
and the method cannot tell them apart on its own. That is a genuine hole. The
best answer available so far is that a parallel organisation tests which is
which empirically rather than by argument, and that only works for the first
category.

## Gaps

- Are the three categories his final cut, or does state regulation deserve its
  own? He folds law into "external factors" and it behaves differently again.
- The Atlassian example names his employer and its compliance posture in a
  public repo. His words and mild, but he should decide whether it stays
  attributed, gets anonymised to "a large enterprise", or comes out.
- What does the cost-to-ignore column look like for a person rather than a
  company? A household cannot eat a lawsuit.
