# Everyone is time poor, so never ask for their time

**Nobody will gratuitously give you their time.** So the ask is never "help me
analyse this problem". The ask is "I am doing the work, it is my time going into
it, and I would like your two cents." **Same information out of them, a fraction
of the cost, and it recruits their experience instead of borrowing their
calendar.**

## In his words

> This is one of my core principles of collaboration. The reality on the ground
> is that everyone is time starved. Everyone is time poor. That means that when
> you find a problem, no one is going to gratuitously give you their time.
> from: 2026-09-09-collaboration-kernel

> You want to tell them, hey, I'm going to work on this problem. Not you, me.
> It's my time that's going to go into it. But while I'm looking into this, I'd
> like to get your two cents on these things. What do you think is the problem?
> What sort of issues have you seen in the past? Is there anything you want to
> share with me, so that I can take your experience and your thoughts into
> consideration in the future?
> from: 2026-09-09-collaboration-kernel

## Stories and evidence

**His three cases, which are the whole argument.** When you go to someone with a
problem, exactly one of three things is true:

1. You have an extremely good case for them, so they are already pre-engaged and
   will be glad you are on it.
2. They are already working on it, so their time is already allocated.
3. **They will give you very little.** Case three is the default and most
   collaboration advice is written as though it were case one.

**The settings pages.** A legacy admin surface, hundreds of pages, seven
standards where there could be one. The scope of the conversation is huge, which
means the cost of the meeting version of this ask is huge too, which is exactly
why nobody grants it.

**What this replaces.** The standard advice is "get alignment early", which says
nothing about what the ask costs the person granting it. This one prices the
ask and then makes it cheap.

## Caveats and edges

He does not say what happens when the two cents are wrong, or when the person
you asked is the one blocking you. He also does not name a cost to the approach,
and there is an obvious one: it is slower, and it puts all the analytical load
on you by design.

The three questions he uses are all backward looking. "What do you think is the
problem" and "what have you seen in the past" gather diagnosis, not policy.

## Gaps

- Where does this fail? A genuine emergency, a hostile stakeholder, someone who
  is themselves the diagnosis.
- Does the ask scale? Three people in a hospital office is not thirty people
  across four teams. Is there a headcount where it breaks?
- How much of his own time is he assuming the asker has? The principle offloads
  everyone else's scarcity onto one person.
