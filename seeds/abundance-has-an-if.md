# Abundance has an if

Every abundance claim has the shape **verb + fill in the blank**. You can learn
X. You will be able to cure Y. The claim is incomplete: the real structure is
**verb + fill in the blank + if**. The conditions are the whole argument, and
they are the part nobody says out loud.

## In his words

> There's actually a corollary to it. There's a part that follows, and this is
> the part that's really the most interesting and where I think a lot of the
> conversation really gets stuck. And it's that it should be verb plus fill in
> the blank, plus **if**.
> from: 2026-09-01-abundance-is-conditional

> Today, you can buy anything. Anything is literally anything. If.
> from: 2026-09-01-abundance-is-conditional

## Stories and evidence

**Buying anything today**, unpacked into its conditions: if you have enough
money, if there is stock available, if you're close enough to the stock to put
your hands on it, if there is a supply chain that can get it to you, if it's
legal where you live, if you're old enough, if you hold the certificate that
allows you to possess it. His example: guns in Spain.

**Curing anything tomorrow**, unpacked the same way: if I have the monetary
resources to request the service, and I have access to the cure, and I live in a
country where it's legal, and where it's produced, and it's available at a price
affordable to my pocket.

The second list is the first list with the nouns swapped. That is the point.

**He does not care about his own list of ifs**, and said so plainly a week
later: "it was just a top of mind first thing that came to mind, it's not
comprehensive, it's not conclusive". The frame is the contribution, the list is
an illustration of it. Anything downstream should treat the ifs as a
demonstration and not as a taxonomy of his settled view.

## His condition categories, adopted 2026-09-08

Five kinds of condition, offered as a starting set rather than a closed list, so
that a reader runs the method on their own domain instead of arguing with the
categories:

| Category | The question it asks |
|---|---|
| Price | Can you pay for it |
| Permission | Is it legal for you, here, from that provider |
| Proximity | Can it physically reach you, or you it |
| Availability | Does it exist near you at all |
| Capability | Do you have the time, foundation and attention to use it |

Discretion sits underneath availability and behaves differently enough to be
worth splitting out: "does someone keep choosing to provide it" is a condition
that can be withdrawn on a quarterly earnings call, which is the flaw the red
team found in the first draft's remedy.

**Capability is the one that matters most to his argument**, because it is the
condition technology is worst at removing, and it is the one the education
promise turns on. Money, permission and access can all be solved and "learn
anything" still fails on time, foundation and attention.

## The worked example, chosen 2026-09-08

One promise, run all the way through, explicitly labelled a demonstration of the
method rather than an argument about the domain: **"you will be able to see a
doctor about anything, instantly, for free."** Price collapses genuinely, and he
gives them that. Permission does not: the model cannot prescribe, refer or order
a scan in almost any jurisdiction. Proximity does not: imaging, blood draws and
surgery need a building and a trained pair of hands within reach. Availability
depends on someone continuing to fund it. Capability decides whether knowing you
are ill turns into being treated.

He wants it centred on the United States, because that is where the Moonshot
audience lives, with two sentences of contrast against the systems he has
actually lived under. That contrast is his to write from experience. The
structural detail underneath it is research and belongs in `research/` with
sources, per his own provenance rule.

## Caveats and edges

He is not arguing against abundance. He says plainly "we really do want to have
this world of abundance". The complaint is about an incomplete sentence, not a
false one.

The frame's own weak joints are now documented in their own seeds:
`tell-me-your-conditions` answers the "it is only a restatement" charge,
`you-cannot-sequence-the-conditions` concedes the order cannot be planned, and
`conditions-have-owners` carries the hole that naming a condition legitimises
it.
