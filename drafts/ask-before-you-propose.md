# Ask Before You Propose

## 🦅 TL;DR

- Big ambiguous problems have no edges, so the instinct is to jump to the first
  idea that feels right. **For a small problem that instinct is correct. For a
  big one it is how the conversation dies.**
- Richard Rumelt's kernel of good strategy - diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent
  action - is taught as a way to think. **I think it is also the order in which
  you are allowed to talk to people.**
- **Nobody owes you their time.** So stop asking for it. "I am doing this work,
  it is my time going into it, I would like your two cents" is a much cheaper
  ask, and you still get their experience.
- **People need to be heard, not obeyed.** You can land somewhere other than
  where they pointed, as long as you can say why. That is what buys you
  sponsorship at approval time.
- When you move into leading, **nobody hands you the frame any more.** Go and
  borrow it from whoever owns the principles. If they have not got any, you have
  not hit a wall, you have found a gap, and you are the one holding the evidence.

---

## 🌀 The problem with no edges

Someone close to me is a designer, moving from individual contributor towards
leading. She has been bringing me problems lately, and they are the good kind of
horrible: part organisational, part strategic, mostly about people.

Here is the one we have been chewing on. **A redesign of the settings and
administration pages of a large legacy product.** Hundreds of pages. And the
moment you start looking properly, you find heaps of things that could be
improved. The indentation. The position of the buttons. Consistency across
pages. **There seem to be seven standards for something that could be one.**

The scope of that conversation is just huge. There are no edges to it.

And when a problem has no edges, the natural thing happens: you jump to the idea
that comes to mind. The classic. *My gut is telling me we should solve it this
way.*

I want to be fair to that instinct, because most advice is not. **When the
problem is small, the first idea usually is the answer.** It is obvious to
someone, they implement it, it is gone in a few minutes, and going through any
kind of process would have cost more than the problem was worth. That is fine.
Do that.

**The trouble starts when the problem is big enough that the foundations
matter** - when you actually need to understand what you are dealing with, and
what principles any solution is going to sit on top of.

## 🔒 How the conversation locks down

So you have looked at the seven standards, you have a feeling about it, and you
go and find someone. And you say:

> Hey, I have seen all of this, and I am thinking we should do blah. What do you
> think?

Two sentences, and you have already lost.

Because you have handed them a gut proposal and nothing else. The only move
available to them is to challenge it, so they do, and now **the conversation is
a referendum on your first idea instead of an examination of the problem.** It
locks down, very quickly, and once it has locked down you will never get the
diagnosis conversation you actually needed.

I do not think this is a work thing, either. Someone in my family is a
paediatric surgeon. She shares a very small office with three other surgeons and
she wanted to make it better. Every single time she proposed something, she got
pushback. She was hammering her head against it.

| The move | What the other person can do with it |
|---|---|
| "I think we should do blah, what do you think?" | Agree, or attack it. Usually attack it. |
| "I am looking into this. What do you think the problem is?" | Tell you something you did not know. |

## 🧠 The kernel, and the part nobody mentions

The thing I keep coming back to is the kernel of good strategy, from Rumelt's
*Good Strategy, Bad Strategy*. Three parts:

> **Diagnosis.** What is actually going on here.
> **Guiding policy.** The approach we are taking, and the boundaries we will not
> cross.
> **Coherent action.** What we do, in what order.

Most people skip straight to the third one, which is the jumping-to-ideas reflex
with a longer run-up.

But here is the bit I want to add, because I do not think it gets said. **The
kernel is normally read as an analysis tool. It is a communication tool at
exactly the same time.** Not in the sense of the words you use or how you talk.
In a harder sense than that:

**If you come to someone at the wrong stage, with content that is not mature
enough for that point in time, you will not be received the same way as if you
had engaged with them through the process itself.**

The kernel is not just how you get to a good answer alone. It is the order in
which you bring other people in. Call it the collaboration enablement side of
the kernel. Which is what the rest of this is about.

## ⏳ Nobody owes you their time

This is one of my core principles of collaboration, and everything else sits on
top of it.

**The reality on the ground is that everyone is time starved.** Everyone is time
poor. Which means that when you find a problem, nobody is going to gratuitously
give you their time. When you go and knock on someone's door, exactly one of
three things is true:

1. **You have an extremely good case for them.** They are already pre-engaged,
   and they will love that you are the one taking this on.
2. **They are already working on it.** Their time is allocated to this problem
   anyway.
3. **They will give you very little.** ⬅️ This is the default, and most advice
   is written as though it were the first one.

The usual guidance here is "get alignment early", and I have never found that
useful, because **it says nothing about what the ask costs the person you are
asking.** So price it, then make it cheap.

The move is to tell them, explicitly, that this is not landing on them:

> I am going to work on this problem. Not you, me. It is my time that is going
> into it. But while I am looking into this, I would like to get your two cents
> on a few things. What do you think the problem is? What sort of issues have
> you seen in the past? Is there anything you want to share with me, so I can
> take your experience and your thoughts into consideration?

Read that again and notice what you are asking for. **Not their time. Their
experience.** One is a calendar negotiation, the other is a two-minute
conversation they will probably enjoy.

## 👂 Heard, not obeyed

Now, why does this work, beyond being polite?

Two reasons, and the second one is the one people miss.

The first is straightforward. **If I know that at some point I am going to need
approval, sponsorship or air cover from somebody, I want to loop that person in
as early as possible.** Not because it is nice, because it is how you get early
engagement and, honestly, early pre-approval. Collaboration has to be end to
end. Asking someone for input and then never showing them where it went is not
half of this practice, it is a different and worse practice.

The second is the interesting one, and it is what I keep telling people who are
nervous about this:

> **Getting someone's input makes them feel listened to. It does not mean the
> solution has to follow what they said.**

People like to be listened to. If you do not follow their guideline, but you
listened to them properly first and you can explain why you eventually landed
somewhere else, **that is usually better than turning up with a solution and
never having talked to them at all.**

So: be conscious of their time, and acknowledge their input and their
experience. Those two things are the foundation of a good early evaluation, and
they are also the foundation of a successful final approval. Same two things,
doing double duty.

## 🧭 Where the frame comes from when nobody hands you one

Here is what I think is actually going on underneath all of this, and why it
tends to bite hardest at a particular moment in a career.

**As an individual contributor, the frame arrives with the problem.** Someone
tells you what to do, or points you at something, very likely with a
pre-solution already attached, or at minimum hands you a frame to work inside.

**When you move into leading, defining that frame becomes the job** - yours and
your lead colleagues'. And nobody announces this. You just find that the move
that worked for years, receive frame and produce a solution inside it, has
quietly stopped being available.

That guiding policy layer is where things get really interesting. The values,
the core, how solutions should be built, which boundaries we are not crossing.
And do not assume the conversation has already happened. When those principles
are already clear, this is easy, but that clarity comes from someone having sat
down and talked about it. **A young lead team usually has not.**

So where do you get one? **You go and borrow it.**

In her case, that is the design system. She has a connection to the design
system team, so the question is: *do you have design principles that any
solution I build needs to fit inside?*

And here is the good part. **If the answer is no, that is not a dead end. You
have just identified a gap in the design system, and you are the one standing
there with the analysis that proves it.** You can go to that team with the
evaluation you have already done, work with them to devise the standards and
principles for any future solution, and then that gets shared far wider than
your problem.

Notice what that does to your position. **You are no longer a consumer of the
system asking permission. You are a contributor to it** - which is a much better
place to be standing, especially when you are new to leading.

It also composes with the time-poverty principle exactly. The design system team
is time poor too. You are not asking them to come and help you redesign
settings. You are handing them evidence about their own system that they could
not easily have gathered themselves.

One thing that is load-bearing here: **this is a higher level conversation, and
it has to be held before any solutions take place.** Turn up after you have a
design and the same meeting becomes a review, and reviews are where proposals go
to be challenged.

## 🪜 A note on altitude

One trap worth naming, because it is where these conversations get eaten.

Seven standards down to one sounds like a big strategic decision. It is not.
**That is a call of execution, and a process of execution.** Mixing it into the
principles conversation drags the high level discussion down into a numbers
argument, and a room arguing about numbers never gets to a principle.

The layering, on our actual problem:

| Layer | The settings redesign |
|---|---|
| **Analysis** | There are seven navigation patterns across hundreds of pages |
| **Guiding principle** | Any solution abides by the design system |
| **Execution** | Seven down to two, on a timeline we choose |

The principle is *the solution needs to abide with the design system*. Then you
start digging: for navigation patterns, what is the correct pattern? The design
system should guide us there. If it has a set of patterns, great, and maybe the
answer comes out as two options we can follow rather than one.

**And then the pace belongs to execution, where partial credit is allowed.** If
we go across all those hundreds of pages and get deviation from seven down to
two, great. If we do it progressively and get from seven down to six this
quarter, that might also be good, from an execution and strategic timeline
perspective. That statement would be incoherent at the principles layer and it
is completely sane at the execution one.

## 🤔 "So am I just a secretary?"

This is the objection I get most, and it is a fair one.

*How does any of this put me in the lead position? I am just asking questions
and gathering everyone else's information. I am not putting my own input in at
all.*

Look at who ends up holding what. **Everyone else has a fragment.** Their bit of
the problem, the thing they saw last year, the pattern that annoys them. **You
are the only person holding all of it, and synthesis is the scarce thing here.
Opinions are not scarce at all.**

There is also this: the person who frames the question owns the shape of the
answer. Deciding what counts as the problem is more power than proposing a
solution ever was.

But the objection is half right, and the half it gets right matters. **If you
gather and never synthesise, you really are a secretary.** So the next move is
not optional:

**Write the diagnosis down and put your name on it.** Not "here is what you all
said". *Here is what I think is going on, given what you told me.* One
paragraph, sharp enough to be wrong. A diagnosis that offends nobody is just a
summary.

And notice the ask has changed shape. It has stopped being *what do you think?*
and become *does this hold?* Different question, different posture, and you have
earned the right to it.

## ⚖️ Where this does not apply

If there were only upsides I would be selling you something.

- **It is slower.** Deliberately. If the building is on fire, propose the thing
  and argue later.
- **It puts all the analytical load on one person by design.** That person is
  you. The principle offloads everyone else's scarcity onto your desk, and you
  should go in knowing that.
- **It assumes the principles-owner exists and is reachable.** Not everyone has
  a design system team, or an architecture group, or anyone who owns the
  standards.
- **Telling a team their system has a hole is only a gift if they receive it
  that way.** Read the room.
- **This only covers two thirds of the kernel.** Everything above is diagnosis
  and guiding policy. What collaboration looks like during coherent action, when
  it becomes execution and coordination, is a different conversation and I have
  not written it down yet.

## ✅ Quick tips

Things to try on your next big ambiguous problem:

- **Delete the proposal from your opening message.** Whatever you were about to
  suggest, hold it. Ask what they think the problem is instead.
- **Say the words "not you, me".** Out loud. It changes what people hear the ask
  as.
- **Use the three questions.** *What do you think the problem is? What issues
  have you seen before? Anything you want to make sure I take into account?*
- **List who will need to approve this**, and talk to every one of them before
  you have an answer, not after.
- **Go and ask whoever owns the principles whether they have any.** Either you
  get your frame, or you get a gap worth collaborating on.
- **Write one paragraph of diagnosis and sign it.** Sharp enough to be wrong.
- **Before you argue a number, ask which layer it belongs to.**

## 🍽️ Food for thought

The thing I find genuinely nice about this is that it is not a technique.

**You honestly could not have written that diagnosis without them.** Their
fingerprints are all over it, and the shape of it is still yours, and those two
things are not in tension at all. Saying so is not a move, it is just true,
which is exactly why it survives contact with people who would smell a move
coming a mile off.

Ask before you propose. Most of the pushback stops being pushback.
