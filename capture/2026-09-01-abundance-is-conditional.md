# Abundance is conditional, and nobody is talking about the conditions

**Context.** Fer's first rant into the repo, spoken. Prompted by the Moonshot
podcast's ongoing conversations about abundance. He wants this written up as a
piece he can share back with the podcast crew and use to push them to dig into
the part he thinks they are skipping.

## The pattern he noticed

> I'm starting a new idea flow here after thinking a lot about the conversations
> that are happening in the Moonshot podcast.

A lot of these podcasts are talking about abundance. Every time, they ask: what
should we do? What does abundance really look like? How do we transition to it?
How do we minimise the issues that are going to come from the path to get there?
Because we really do want to have this world of abundance. But there's still a
lot of doubt around what we're going to do to get there, and how we can overcome
all the fears that exist around AI.

When they talk about abundance for everyone, what he'd really like to think
about is: it's not that everyone can have anything, or access everything, or
fulfil all their dreams.

One of the things they call out consistently is that the future is for
entrepreneurs. But not everyone's an entrepreneur, and there are long
discussions around that. The bit he finds more interesting is when they talk
about **extreme agency**. You will be able to do anything. You will be able to
access anything.

> There starts to be a pattern there, which is what I would like to explore,
> because what I'm hearing is: you can do, fill in the blank. You can learn,
> fill in the blank. You will be able to cure, fill in the blank. You will be
> able to discover, fill in the blank.

The structure of that future is: **everyone can, a verb, fill in the blank.**

Within that structure it all sounds great, because it's a world of promise and
it sounds awesome. So I can learn Chinese. I can learn how to swim. I can learn
how to roller skate. All those things are all well and fine.

## The corollary nobody says out loud

But there's a very strong aspect he is not hearing the Moonshot podcast mates
talk about. This is the thing he'd like to write a bit of a dissertation on, and
actually ask them to dig into it.

> It's the fact that in this pattern of verb plus fill in the blank, there's
> actually a corollary to it. There's a part that follows, and this is the part
> that's really the most interesting and where I think a lot of the conversation
> really gets stuck. And it's that it should be verb plus fill in the blank,
> plus **if**.

We live in a world of conditions.

Today, you can buy anything. Anything is literally anything. **If.** And here
are the conditions:

- if you have enough money
- if there is stock available
- if you're close enough to the stock that you can actually put your hands on it
- if there is a supply chain that can actually get it to you

All these conditions need to be met. Additionally, depending on where you live,
there might be more:

- if it's legal where you live
- if you're old enough, so it's legal for your age range
- if you've passed a particular test, if you have a certificate that allows you
  to have that in your possession. Think about guns in Spain, for example.

There are a lot of these conditions, and that's really where the matter comes
into play.

## The money conditional

When we talk about a world of abundance, you hear Elon Musk talk about this
sometimes, and you hear people say: money won't matter. In the future economy,
money won't matter. We'll have to reset the money somehow. Musk says the same
thing: everyone will have high basic income, and working will be optional.

Well, if you take that away, then that's the biggest conditional that right now
we live our lives upon. We can do things if we have access to the financial
resources to do them or acquire them.

> A financial resource is really, really important for us to be part of that
> action, like verb plus fill in the blank. If we don't have money, we can't do
> these things.

And it's very hard for people to grasp a world where there's no money. So we
need to gradually find a way for people to continue to participate in the
economy, with a token of exchange. But we have to acknowledge that that
condition is there.

## The national conditional

The second part, which is really interesting, is that we have to look in the
eyes the fact that there are many other conditions that are country based,
government based.

You hear the Moonshot mates talk a little about America winning the AI race. And
careful with China. We need to compete with China. We need to beat China.

> The reality is that if we retain these countries, the countries will continue
> to impose their own ifs, as the technology disperses around the world.

China has electric vehicles, America has electric vehicles, Europe has electric
vehicles, but they all have them under their own ifs, under their own
conditions. The technology will continue to disperse, same as AI right now
across the entire world, but the use of it or the value of it falls within the
conditions of what those countries allow to be done with it. That same thing
will continue to happen.

So each of the ifs will continue to be located to a particular country, until
the countries disappear. And countries are not going to disappear anytime soon.
That's going to take a fair bit of time in the transition.

<!-- NOTE: half-formed, and he knows it. Kept as said. -->

> We'll have to start with some sort of, I don't know, exodus from Earth. Maybe
> we have to start new and we'll start new countries, with our same if
> conditions, on the moon or on Mars.

But once we start talking about those parts of the equation, then we can really
start to understand how we can potentially transition to this world.

## Why money doesn't go away

In the economy, people need to have money to be able to participate in it. If AI
and robotics take over labour, humans stop having an income. They stop having
the resource that allows them to fulfil the ability to do things.

So you could say: well, if we give everything for free, they don't need to have
money.

> Which doesn't make any sense to me. There's always going to be some sort of
> value attributed to things. Not everything is worth nothing. At least we're
> not going to be there any time soon.

Also, the groups of people and companies that come together to actually offer
these services and goods will do so in exchange for something. So we have to
assume there's still going to be some coin of trade in order to obtain things,
be it knowledge or services or what have you.

> Even if you were given a ration system, the rationing is still based on some
> sort of token that you get on a regular basis.

So let's work under the assumption that money is still around. Under that
premise, money is given to people who produce something of value to someone. So
people need to be producing something in order to get money. Either that, or
there's a flow of money.

In a world where people don't work but money still needs to flow, there needs to
be some sort of input of money for them, so they can continue to participate in
the economy and therefore have the money flow back into the businesses that
produce services and goods.

## Not through tax

Once the money reaches these companies, we could fall in the trap of saying:
well, it will flow back to the people through governments and taxes.

> That makes no sense to me. I don't think tax is a reasonable way for the goods
> and services to then flow through the government and then back to the people.

Position ourselves in a country where tax is held stable, 10%, you pay what you
pay, and that funds the government and whatever the government manages. But it's
the companies that actually have the leverage. They have the resource for
exchange, because the money ends up flowing to the companies' bank accounts.

There is a fear that there's going to be a very high centralisation of power to
a very few. So we're going to have very few people harnessing a vast, vast
majority of the world's economic power. There's going to be very few that are
very, very rich.

This is obviously problematic, because if they hold the money, how do they make
it flow back to everyone else?

## What the displaced would do

Position ourselves in a world where 20, 30, 40% of the population, over the
course of a few years, starts losing productive value when contrasted with
technology, and the technology's ability to produce the same value for a lot
less, much faster and with higher quality.

There's so many things we could have these people do. We could have them running
courses for others, smaller services that have no productive value from a
producing-goods perspective. So we just expand the services we offer one
another:

- health and training sessions, group training, humans training humans,
  specialists in group training and personal trainers
- sexual exchanges and company services
- entertainment: theatre, music
- all the arts that produce well-being

> We produce human well-being and sensations, physical and emotional well-being.

Those will be the services we can imagine many people could be paid for.

Now, of course, they have to be paid for. And the people who attend these, to
enjoy these, need to have something to pay with. So we're going back to: what
would they pay with if they don't have any money? And these are, of course,
secondary in the Maslow pyramid. They first have to pay their house, their
loans, their children's education, their health.

> Even if we solve all cures, we're going to have to pay for health.

Going back to the formula: I will be able to heal all my pains, **if** I have
the monetary resources to request those services, **and** I have access to those
cures, **and** I live in a country where they are legal, **and** where they are
produced, **and** they are made available to me at a price that's affordable to
my pocket. All those conditions need to be met.

## How the money flows back: Belgium vs Spain

The companies need to be the ones that start promoting that their money flows
back to their consumers. They can do that in two ways.

**Belgium.** You go to a hospital, and they make it very clear to you how much
it costs to pay the hospital services, and you pay them, and then you go back to
your local social services and they pay you back. You make the money flow
through the system. Ultimately the users don't have a cost, but they are well
aware of how much things cost, or at least the value we can attribute to certain
things, so they have a good reference of contrast.

**Spain.** You go to a hospital and you don't pay anything. Everything is free
from a consumer's perspective, after having paid tax to sustain the system, of
course.

And of course you've got cases where you need that insurance. Say you're
Belgian and you don't have the money. You still get the service, and you can see
on the bill how much it would have cost you if you had been able to pay it.

## The question is for the companies, not the governments

The money has to flow out of those companies. And those companies then have a
very interesting choice, and that's where he thinks we really should focus.

> We shouldn't be talking about what the government should do. I hear the
> Moonshot mates talking, what should governments do? No, no, no, no. This is
> what should these huge businesses do.

Let's talk about a business that makes a billion a day, like Nvidia, two billion
a day, like Google. What should they be doing with that cash flow?

     this goes anywhere public. See meta/backlog.md. -->

They have to sustain the cost of their services. If the cost of running their
services is reduced over time, thanks to automation and cheaper energy
production and cheaper chip production and maintenance of the overall systems,
mechanical systems, production systems, material extraction, then great. They'll
have more profits on their balance sheet.

Ultimately we're talking about: what do they do with their profits?

We want them to sustain those services, because that is the foundation of the
actual value of abundance that we're going towards. So those services need to be
sustained, and we accept as a society that the investment to sustain it remains.
So we only look at the profit balance sheet. That, of course, includes the
employees' cost, while they have employees.

Once you look at the profits, the question is: what should those companies be
doing with those billions and billions of profits? If you think about only one
company, then maybe that's not enough. But if you think about the top five, like
the S&P 500, and you put a collective of all their profits on the table.

## The reinvestment counter-argument

Each of these companies looked at in isolation is going to have its own
investment path. You talk with them and they're going to say: well, we're going
to reinvest these profits into expanding our business and improving our services
even more, and breaking the frontier. So the money can't go back to the
consumers just yet.

The way they could frame it is: we are sending the money back to society by
giving new people jobs. We're going to start new businesses. We're going to
start new avenues of investment where we're going to expand our business by
building more data centres. We're creating jobs by building new energy power
plants, giving people more jobs.

So they could always say that they're bringing back the money to the economy
through offering new roles. At least for now, and sometimes that might disappear.
Or they could be lowering the cost of their products, still offering the
services, but charging less because they're more efficient in their process.

## How much is enough?

> I think really that is the big discussion that needs to be had. How much money
> can a company accrue? And if you think about it from an owner's perspective,
> how much money can one person accrue?

This of course opens the door to socialist discussions. That money is well
earned by the founders of a massive business. It's theirs by right. Should you
be able to take it away? Should someone be able to cap how much money someone
owns, and by extension how much access they have to wealth? Is there a limit
after which...

> I'm part of the, let's say, middle upper class, well-placed person, but I don't
> have access to endless luxuries. I do have to work every day. I can't retire
> whenever I want to, hypothetically. But I imagine there is a threshold. I can
> put a threshold in my mind through which I would be able to not need to work
> the rest of my life if I didn't want to.

That could very much be the edge we can start talking about. When Elon Musk says
working will be optional, that is already the case for a very small subset of
humanity, because they have already accrued so much wealth that even the wealth
itself, if they did nothing with it, would continue to generate value. Even if
they just put it bluntly in a bank account, and accounted for inflation for the
next 100 years, living an above average lifestyle, they'd still not run out.

> So how much is enough for someone to own in order to make working optional?
> Maybe that is a threshold that we should be talking about, because if we can
> expand the number of people that live in that threshold today, we can
> gradually expand the number of people that can live in that world.

Now those people should, in principle, be the ones that lose their job. So if
you're going to lose your job in the next three to five years and you're going
to be completely replaced, and we're going to be moving to a world where you
don't need to work, you should at least have enough money to sustain you for the
rest of your life so that you don't have to work if you don't want to.

## Everything is limited

This opens another completely different door, which is: what is the base quality
of life that that would imply? Because it's too easy to say, oh, you can have
anything.

> No one can have anything. Everything is limited. Materials are limited. Atoms
> are limited. Access to energy is limited, and it will continue to be limited
> for many years. Compute is limited. Time is very limited.

Even if we start curing everything, the number of cures available will be
limited. The number of drinks distilled will be limited. The number of bottles
of Cabernet Sauvignon of a special reserve of, I don't know, 15, 20 years ago,
will be limited. You can't make more of those.

So defining the thresholds of what a good lifestyle is for humanity as a whole,
that's close to impossible.

## Companies live under conditions too

If we assume that companies will continue to try to remain competitive, hence
reinvesting their own profits into themselves and into their markets to continue
to improve and not lose traction against others, because generating money for
the future to be sustainable in the long run is still a hard requirement of the
conditions that the business producing goods and services works under.

A company can produce anything for as long as they want, as long as they can
seem to have customers that pay them for it, and they continue to have supply
chains that supply them the innovations and products they need to offer those
services and products.

> They're still conditions. And one of the big conditions is they will continue
> to have those customers **if** there's nothing better in the market, and if
> they continue to innovate and continue to offer the solution their customers
> require in the modern times they live in.

If that condition isn't met, that company will die. And the profits will move
someplace else, because that's where people will continue to invest their time
and get their value from.

## In short

> Abundance sounds great, but everything is conditioned. Conditions are vastly
> defined by national governments and civilisation rules within geographic
> boundaries that we've defined over years and years. And within all those
> boundaries, there is a condition of token of exchange.

That token of exchange will continue to live. We can't assume it's going away
any time soon, because it is the foundation on which the companies that offer
services and products for the abundance to exist rely, and the foundation they
rely on to understand whether they can continue offering those services or not,
whether they continue to compete, and get the best talent and the best services
and the energy they require in order to continue to offer that.

> We don't live in a globalised world under a single nation where humanity is
> understood to be one. We still see a lot of separation between nations, and we
> hear that from a competitive standpoint, again, going back to our lovely
> Moonshot mates. We have to beat China. We have to beat China. We hear them say
> that again and again and again. I'm far from hearing them uniting forces on a
> humanity standard, or even trying.

People need to be part of the economy. They need to continue accessing the
resources they need in order to fulfil the exchange and the participation
conditions to be able to attain that abundance. In order to do that, those that
accrue the resources required need to make those flow, in a way that fulfils any
condition those humans might be in. Including being displaced from their work.

## So what instead?

> Does that mean we need UBI? I don't think so.

Maybe we need these huge companies to actually start service businesses where
they offer services for free.

> So maybe we have the Tesla gyms, and the SpaceX theatres, and the OpenAI movie
> making industry, and the Claude medical services, and all of these are free of
> charge for everyone, anyone in the world, anywhere in the world.

That's the way we can actually attain a means of having everyone be able to
participate in the economy, by truly bringing service down to zero cost.

> But there has to be a choice made by the enterprises, not by the government.
> The enterprises are going to be waiting for the governments to make their
> choice for them. While they fight to remain competitive between them, then
> we're in a deadlock.

> You've got the resources, you've got to make the choice. The resources accrue
> to you, you've got to make the choice. You can't be the naughty kid that keeps
> everything to themselves until the parent comes along and says, stop hogging
> the ball, share it.
