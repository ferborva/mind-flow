# The strategy kernel as a collaboration framework

**Context.** Voice, in conversation, and deliberately not the abundance thread.
He opened it by saying so: this is a second brain dump he wants running in
parallel. The trigger was his wife, a designer at Moodle moving from individual
contributor towards lead, coming to him with an ambiguous redesign problem and
people problems attached to it. He set the register up front - "imagine we're at
a bar", push back, challenge, add facts - and then walked through how he coaches
people through large ambiguous problems using Rumelt's kernel from *Good
Strategy, Bad Strategy*.

**Fidelity note.** Voice transcription, so heavier cleaning than usual. Filler
removed, sentences closed, and a short list of obvious mis-transcriptions
corrected: "colonel" to kernel, "quantum attack" to a phrase about horizons that
did not survive, "I see the password" to I see the pattern, "do a Feb" to do a
third, "buzz synthesizer" to synthesiser. Everything else is his, artefacts
included. The garbled turns are marked where they matter, because a garbled turn
is not a licence to guess what he meant. Verbatim at
`capture/raw/2026-09-09-collaboration-kernel.md`.

**Second-voice note.** This was a dialogue, not a monologue, and some of the
material below is his agreement with something Claude said rather than something
he said first. Those turns are marked. Claude's em dashes are normalised to his

## Where it starts: jumping to the answer

The reflex he is trying to interrupt, in his words:

> The natural action that comes to her is jumping to the ideas that come to
> mind. The classic, hey, first idea that comes to mind, my gut feel is telling
> me that I should solve it this way.

He is careful not to make this a sin. Small problems deserve the reflex:

> Sometimes when you've got a small problem to deal with, the answer is
> "obvious" to someone, and they just can implement it and get it out of the way
> in a few minutes. And sometimes that's fine.

The line is size:

> But when you're dealing with a bigger problem, most of the time you really
> want to understand the problem in depth, and what sort of foundational
> principles you're building on top.

His spine for that is Rumelt: **diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action.**

## The pushback: the kernel is a communication tool

Claude's challenge was that the kernel is an analysis tool, not a communication
one, and that a crisp diagnosis delivered cold can land as "you're all wrong".
He rejected it, and this is the sharpest turn in the conversation:

> I'm going to push back on your pushback. The kernel, while it's not a
> communication tool, at the same time, because, I mean, not a communication
> tool in the sense of how you talk or the words you use - the reality is that
> it is, at the same time, exactly that.

The mechanism, in his words:

> If you come to someone and present to them, or communicate to them, at a wrong
> stage or too early phase, with content that is not mature enough or is not
> ready for that point in time, you will not be received by the listener the
> same way as if you actually engage with them through the kernel's process
> itself.

And the failure it produces:

> That conversation would quickly lock down very, very easily, when someone just
> starts challenging your first gut proposal.

He names what he is reaching for and does not fully define it:

> At least in the kernel from a collaboration standpoint too. There is a
> collaboration aspect, a collaboration enablement side to the kernel. Maybe
> that's what we're going to talk about today.

## The worked example: seven standards

The concrete case, which he returns to throughout:

> She's dealing with a really big problem of a redesign of the settings and
> administration pages of a website. This is a legacy system, really, really
> complex. And the moment you start looking into something, you start finding
> heaps of things that could be improved. The indentation, the position of the
> buttons, consistency across pages. There's hundreds of pages. There seem to be
> seven standards for something that could be one, and the scope of the
> conversation is just huge.

The wrong opening move, in his words:

> So say, for example, they come and say, hey, I've seen all of this, and I'm
> thinking we should do blah. What do you think?

## Principle one: everyone is time poor

He calls this one out explicitly as his own:

> This is one of my core principles of collaboration. The reality on the ground
> is that everyone is time starved. Everyone is time poor. That means that when
> you find a problem, no one is going to gratuitously give you their time.

The three cases he enumerates. Either you have an extremely good case and they
are already pre-engaged and "gonna love the fact that you're engaging in this
problem with them", or they are already working on it and their time is already
allocated, "or they're gonna give you very little".

So the ask changes shape:

> You want to tell them, hey, I'm going to work on this problem. Not you, me.
> It's my time that's going to go into it. But while I'm looking into this, I'd
> like to get your two cents on these things. What do you think is the problem?
> What sort of issues have you seen in the past? Is there anything you want to
> share with me, so that I can take your experience and your thoughts into
> consideration in the future?

## Principle two: heard, not obeyed

Claude pushed on closing the loop: input given and never reported back makes
people stingier next time. He agreed and went further:

> When we're talking about collaboration, it has to be a collaboration end to
> end.

The sponsorship logic:

> If I'm working on a problem and I know that whenever we come to a solution
> phase, or even a solution approval phase, I'm going to need approval or
> sponsorship or even support from other people, I want to loop those people in
> as soon as possible to get that early engagement and early pre-approval.

And then the part he flags as the other principle, the one he was telling his
wife:

> What's interesting is that getting their input makes them feel listened to. It
> doesn't mean that the solution necessarily has to follow what they said. And
> this is something I was talking about with my wife. People like to be listened
> to. If you don't follow their guideline, but you've already listened to them
> at the very least, and you can explain why eventually you've landed on
> something else, that is often better than going to a solution without having
> talked to them to start with.

His own summary of the pair:

> Engaging with people, being conscious of their time, number one, and number
> two, acknowledging their input and their value, their experience, are in my
> eyes the key foundations for a good early phase evaluation and a successful
> final phase approval.

## The transition his wife is actually in

This is the frame he puts around the whole conversation:

> As she transitions from individual contributor to leader, she's moving from a
> space where they're telling her what to do, or they're pointing towards a
> problem, very likely with a pre-solution, or they've already given her a frame
> within which to work. And she's moving towards a space where this frame has to
> be defined by her and her lead colleagues.

On the guiding policy layer being where the interesting work now lives:

> That space of those guiding principles, those high level conversations, is
> where things can get really, really interesting.

And a caution about assuming the frame already exists:

> If the guiding principles haven't been discussed previously, if they're very
> clear from a strategic perspective, then that's easy, or done. But that
> usually comes from assuming that people have already talked about this.

Which he does not assume:

> So let's assume that these lead teams, or these young lead teams, haven't
> talked about the guiding principles that they want to stay within. Their
> values, their core, the leaps around how solutions should be built, and which
> boundaries they shouldn't cross.

<!-- NOTE: "the leaps around how solutions should be built" is either a
     transcription artefact or his own phrase. Left as heard. -->

## Where to find a guiding policy: borrow it

His actual advice to her, and the most portable move in the conversation:

> What I pointed her to is the design system itself. Hey, you've got a
> connection to the design system team. Do they have design principles within
> which any solution that you build needs to fit? And if not, then you're likely
> calling out a potential gap in the design system that you'd want to collaborate
> on with the design system team. You can come to them with the evaluation that
> you've done of the problem, and then you can work with them to help them devise
> a set of standards and a set of principles for any future solution, that can
> then be divided out more broadly. And that's a higher level conversation, to be
> held before any solutions take place.

## Principle versus execution

Claude proposed that reducing seven standards to one is a scoping call, a
guiding policy question, and asked whether she has cover to make it. He
disagreed, and drew a cleaner line:

> I wouldn't even go that far. I would actually say that, for example, to the
> point of reducing seven standards down to one, that's a call of execution. And
> a process of execution.

The layering, in his words:

> If from an analysis perspective you've evaluated that there's seven patterns,
> then you talk about the guiding principles for design solutions, the frame
> within which any solution has to live. You could, as a guiding principle in
> this case, say, well, the solution needs to abide with the design system. That
> is a high level principle. And then you start digging and you go, okay, for
> navigation patterns, if there's seven navigation patterns, what is the correct
> navigation pattern? The design system should guide us there. And if the design
> system has a set of patterns, great. And maybe the final answer comes down to,
> well, there's likely these two options that we can follow.

Then the pace, which he places in execution rather than in the policy:

> So if indeed we execute on all these hundreds of pages and reduce the overall
> deviation that there is across patterns from seven down to two, great. If we
> do it progressively and we come from seven down to six, that might also be
> good from an execution perspective, and strategic timeline.

Claude warned that the progressive version tends to ship an eighth pattern
before the old ones die, and asked whether she can retire patterns or only add
better ones. He declined the thread:

> Yeah, now it's talking about execution and coordination. That's not where I
> want to go with this conversation today.

## This is a coaching pattern, not advice for his wife

> I've had this sort of coaching conversation in the past with several people,
> and my wife is just one example I've had recently. But I've also guided other
> people through a similar process where they're trying to face a large and
> ambiguous problem, and they lack a system through which to engage with the
> problem in a structured manner, that enables them to collaborate with high
> level leaders that will be their sponsors in the future, while gradually
> creating a foundation on which to design any solution that can fit.

<!-- NOTE: that one sentence is the whole thesis of the piece, and he produced
     it unprompted. It is also the closest thing in the transcript to an
     abstract. Worth keeping intact. -->

## His sister and the hospital office

Raw material he supplied, and the only other real story in the transcript:

> My sister was talking with me about how she could improve the office where she

> have buy-in from the others. So she wasn't sure what to do, and she was

## The secretary objection

He raised the objection himself, in the voice of someone he would be coaching.
The turn is badly garbled and the gist is clear:

> How does this actually put me in the lead position? I'm just asking questions
> and gathering their information, and not my input. I would raise that in my
> coaching conversation, but telling them that they're actually moving towards a
> lead position.

Claude's answer, in three parts: everyone else holds a fragment and you are the
only one holding all of it, so synthesis is the scarce thing; the person who
frames the question owns the shape of the answer; and the catch is that you have
to land it, because gathering and never synthesising really is being a
secretary. Then, on his "how do you take the next step":

> You write the diagnosis down and put your name on it. Not "here's what you all
> said", but "here's what I think is going on, given what you told me". One
> paragraph, sharp enough to be wrong. And it has to cost something. A diagnosis
> that offends nobody is just a summary. The people you talked to will recognise
> their fingerprints on it, which is why they'll back you rather than fight you.

His endorsement, and this turn is the most damaged in the transcript:

> Gotcha. And actually, it still holds your command. It's not [garbled]
> contribution as the... it's channel support and it's a synthesiser. While you
> maintain, like, a side... the fingerprints from the rest in the way I would go
> allow them.

## On whether it is worth sharing

He asked what Claude thought of it. The answer, which he did not contest: the
kernel is well-trodden ground, the two collaboration principles are the
uncommon part, and the whole thing is currently articulated abstractly, so it
will live or die on whether the settings-page story travels with it.

## Loose ends

- **What he actually said to his sister.** He says "I told her a similar story"
  and never tells it. The transcript substitutes a role play by Claude. His
  version is the missing evidence for the best story in the thread.
- **The collaboration enablement side of the kernel.** He names it and never
  defines it. Is it the two principles, or is there more under it?
- **Pattern retirement.** He parked it as execution. It is the mechanism by
  which the progressive path avoids becoming an eighth standard, and nothing in
  his framework answers it yet.
- **The garbled endorsement.** The secretary answer is Claude's reasoning with
  his agreement attached. Needs restating in his own words before it can be
  published as his.
- **How many people has he run this with, and did it work?** He says several,
  and gives no outcomes. A framework with no failure cases reads as marketing.
- **Does the sequencing ever backfire?** Asking before proposing has a cost:
  it is slower, and in a genuine emergency it may be the wrong move. He does not
  say where the boundary is.
- **Where does coherent action go?** He walks diagnosis and guiding policy in
  detail and repeatedly refuses execution. Is the collaboration layer silent on
  Rumelt's third element, or has he just not got to it?
- **Procedural justice.** Claude cited it unsourced. Verify before any draft
  leans on it.
