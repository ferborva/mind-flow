# The economics his labour paradox is already sitting on

He named a real problem in two lines. It has a literature, and he should know
the names before he puts it in front of the Moonshot crew, because two of them
will supply the names for him.

## 🦅 TL;DR

- **His paradox is the limit case of a standard result.** The reservation wage
  and the backward-bending labour supply curve. If needs are met at zero cost,
  the reservation wage for any purely monetary offer goes to infinity.
- **Baumol's cost disease says his two ideas pull in opposite directions**, and
  that is the most interesting thing in the capture.
- **Knowing the names is defensive, not decorative.** "This is just Baumol" is
  the cheapest possible dismissal of his argument. Naming it first turns that
  from an attack into agreement.

---

## 📉 Reservation wage and backward-bending supply

**Reservation wage.** The lowest wage at which a worker will accept a job. Below
it they take leisure instead.

**Backward-bending labour supply.** Individual labour supply comes from two
opposing forces. The substitution effect makes leisure more expensive as wages
rise, so you work more. The income effect means higher earnings let you hit your
target income with fewer hours, so you work less. When the income effect wins,
the curve bends backward and **people work less as pay rises.**

**His case is the limit.** He removes the target income entirely: if everything
you need is already free, no wage clears a reservation wage that no longer has
anything to buy. The curve does not bend, it detaches.

Worth noting for accuracy: **the market-level labour supply curve typically
slopes upward even where individual curves bend backward**, because new entrants
join. His scenario is unusual precisely because it removes the entrants too.

## 🩺 Baumol's cost disease, and why it breaks the tie

Baumol's mechanism: **wages in low-productivity sectors rise in step with wages
in high-productivity sectors, even though their productivity has not moved.**
Manufacturing gets more efficient, wages rise economy-wide, and the sectors that
cannot automate must pay those wages anyway. So their costs, and their prices,
rise.

The standing evidence is exactly the sectors that matter most: **health,
education, care and live performance get more expensive while manufactured goods
get cheaper.** College and healthcare costs have risen steadily for decades
against falling prices for nearly every physical good.

**This is what makes his two paragraphs collide.**

| His idea | What Baumol predicts |
|---|---|
| Count of products at ~zero cost, rising | True, and accelerating, for anything automatable |
| Products that still need human labour | Get relatively **more** expensive, not less |

So his own two claims predict opposite motions in the same economy. **The
zero-cost count climbs while the price of the human-required residue climbs with
it.**

**That is not a defect in his metric.** It arguably makes it diagnostic. The gap
between the two columns is a direct measure of where residual scarcity is
concentrating, and it would tell a country precisely where its remaining
distributional problem lives. But it does mean the metric cannot be reported as
a single happy number, and he should say so before someone else does.

## 🧭 What this does to the draft

**It supplies the ending the piece did not have.**

The current draft closes on companies choosing to give services away, and the
research showed the reasoning for that was wrong: redistribution is short by a
factor of twenty. The deflation metric replaces it cleanly. **Not "companies
should be generous" but "here are two numbers to be held to".** A target for
businesses, a tracked statistic for countries. That is a genuine
try-it-tomorrow close, which is what the long-form checklist asks for and the
draft currently fakes.

**And the labour paradox supplies the honest caveat**, which the checklist also
asks for. His style guideline is blunt about this: "if there are only upsides to
a change, it's propaganda." The paradox is the downside of his own proposal,
found by him, in the paragraph immediately after proposing it. That is the piece
arguing against itself in public, which is the most persuasive move available.

**One warning.** The Baumol connection cuts both ways. It rescues his labour
paradox by giving it a mechanism, and it complicates his metric by predicting a
column that moves the wrong way. He needs to decide whether the metric is
reported as one number or two. **My read: two, and the gap between them is the
interesting one.** But that is his call, because it is his idea.

## ❓ What I could not settle

- **Whether anyone is already tracking something like his zero-cost count.**
  I did not find a named index for "share of a household basket available at
  near-zero marginal cost". If it does not exist, that is a stronger result for
  him than if it does, but absence of evidence in three searches is not absence.
  Worth a deeper look before he claims novelty.
- **The empirical size of the human-required residue.** How much of a decent
  living standard genuinely cannot be automated is an open question and the
  answer decides whether his paradox is a footnote or the main event.
