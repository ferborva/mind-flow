# What actually binds in four health systems

The worked example in `drafts/name-the-if.md` runs one promise - *you will be
able to see a doctor about anything, instantly, for free* - across Spain,
Belgium, Australia and the United States. Until now that table was lived
experience plus general knowledge. This is the evidence behind it, and **one row
of it was wrong.**

## 🦅 TL;DR

- **The Spain row was wrong, and wrong in an interesting way.** Spain's
  out-of-pocket share of health spending is **20.9%**, *above* Belgium's 17-18%
  and well above the EU average of 14.5%. Price does not bind at the consulting
  room door and then reappears at the pharmacy and the dentist.
- **That correction makes his argument stronger, not weaker.** It is a condition
  handover happening inside a single country: remove the price at the door and
  it turns up two steps downstream, in the thing the doctor tells you to do
  next.
- The other three rows hold up, with better numbers than the draft had.
- **The permission row needs updating for 2026.** "AI cannot prescribe" is still
  broadly true, and it is no longer uniformly true: Delaware has banned AI
  licensure outright, Utah is piloting autonomous prescription renewals, and
  Idaho and Iowa have bills creating a licensed AI provider category. The
  condition is being actively renegotiated right now.
- **The single best number for the piece:** 34.6% of US counties are maternity
  care deserts, with no obstetric clinician and no birthing facility.

## 🇪🇸 Spain: the correction

| Figure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Out-of-pocket, share of total health spending, 2023 | **20.9%** | [World Health Systems Facts, Spain](https://healthsystemsfacts.org/spain/spain-health-system-expenditures/) citing OECD |
| EU average out-of-pocket share, 2021 | 14.5% | [OECD/European Observatory, Spain Country Health Profile 2023](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2023/12/spain-country-health-profile-2023_727a7995/71d029b2-en.pdf) |
| What that out-of-pocket money buys, 2023 | medical goods incl. pharmaceuticals **50%**, dental **32%**, outpatient services 10%, long-term care 6%, inpatient 1% | as above |
| Catastrophic health spending | among the lowest in the EU, despite the high OOP share | as above |

**So the honest version of the Spain row is not "price does not bind".** It is
that price is removed at the point of consultation and returns immediately
afterwards, in medicines and dentistry, which together are 82% of what Spanish
households pay out of pocket. The consultation is free. The consequence of the
consultation is not.

The pharmaceutical co-payment is income-banded, and was reformed this year by
RDL 11/2026. Pensioners on under €18,000 pay 10% capped at **€8.23 a month**;
the top band, over €100,000, pays 60% capped at €61.75 a month. Non-contributory
pensioners, people with recognised disability, recipients of integration income
and unemployed people who have exhausted their subsidy are exempt.
Source: [laboral-social.com on RDL 11/2026](https://www.laboral-social.com/copago-farmaceutico-renta-pacientes-cronicos-pensionistas-vulnerables),
[Generalitat Valenciana, aportación farmacéutica estatal](https://www.san.gva.es/es/web/farmacia-i-productes-sanitaris/ciudadania/aportacion-farmaceutica-estatal).

**What does bind in Spain is availability, and the numbers are large.** At 31
December 2025, **853,509 people** were on a surgical waiting list and **more
than four million** were waiting for a specialist appointment. The average wait
for an outpatient specialist consultation is **102 days**. Surgical waits vary
enormously by region: Andalusia 173 days, Cantabria 137, Extremadura 135, Aragón
132.
Source: [Infobae, 16 April 2026, reporting Ministerio de Sanidad figures](https://www.infobae.com/espana/2026/04/16/las-listas-de-espera-quirurgica-superan-las-850000-personas-en-2025/).
Primary data: [Ministerio de Sanidad, listas de espera](https://www.sanidad.gob.es/estadEstudios/estadisticas/inforRecopilaciones/listaEspera.htm).

## 🇧🇪 Belgium: his felt account is right, the ranking is not

| Figure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Out-of-pocket, share of current health expenditure | **17-18%**, concentrated in dental, outpatient medicines and devices | [Belgian Health System Performance Assessment, indicator A-2](https://www.belgiqueenbonnesante.be/metadata/hspa/2024/A2_A3.pdf) |
| GP consultation co-payment with a Global Medical File and BIM status | **as low as €1** | [callmepower.be, ticket modérateur 2026](https://callmepower.be/fr/assurance/sante/mutuelle/ticket-moderateur) |
| Annual cap on co-payments, social MàF ceiling for BIM households | **~€450 a year**, lower for the lowest incomes | [INAMI, maximum à facturer](https://www.inami.fgov.be/fr/themes/soins-de-sante-cout-et-remboursement/facilites-financieres/maximum-a-facturer-maf) |

**His description of the mechanism is accurate**: you pay, you see the number,
you are reimbursed, and the reference for what things cost survives the
transaction. What is not accurate is the implied ranking. **Belgium's
out-of-pocket share is lower than Spain's**, not higher, so "Belgium shows you
the price, Spain does not" is a statement about visibility rather than about
burden. Worth keeping the distinction, because it is the interesting half: the
Belgian system makes the condition visible while cancelling most of it.

## 🇦🇺 Australia: the row that has moved most

| Figure | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| GPs bulk billing all adult patients, 2026 | **fewer than half** | [Cleanbill Blue Report 2026, via ABC News, 12 April 2026](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-04-12/bulk-billing-doctor-gp-cleanbill-health-medical/106541616) |
| National average out-of-pocket, standard non-bulk-billed GP consult | **$49.23** | as above |
| Metro average | $48.89, up from $42.79 in 2025, **+14.3% in a year** | as above |
| Medium rural towns | $50.25, up from $44.21 | as above |
| Medicare specialist consults in hospital that were bulk billed, 2024-25 | **2.5%** (223,000 services) | [AIHW, referred medical specialist attendances](https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/medical-specialists/referred-medical-specialist-attendances) |
| Patients incurring an out-of-pocket cost for specialist care | **92%**, 2.1 million people, averaging **$272 a year** or $68 a service | as above |
| Very remote areas | average $49 out of pocket per non-bulk-billed GP attendance; bulk billing rates fell in every remoteness category after the 2020-21 peak | [AIHW, bulk billing by remoteness](https://www.aihw.gov.au/reports/medicare/medicare-bulk-billing-of-gp-attendances-over-time/contents/bulk-billing-rates-for-gp-attendances/patterns-in-gp-bulk-billing-rates-by-remoteness) |

**The specialist number is the one to use.** A GP gap of $49 is survivable for
most people. A system where 92% of specialist patients pay, and the referral is
the only route to the scan, the diagnosis and the treatment, is a system where
price binds at exactly the point the promise gets interesting.

**Insurance is not the same as access, and the maternity data is the cleanest
proof of it available.** A county with no obstetric clinician cannot be fixed by
lowering a price, and it is getting worse while the technology gets better,
which is the draft's argument in one dataset.

## ⚖️ Permission: the condition that is actually moving

The draft says the model cannot prescribe, refer or order a scan in almost any
jurisdiction. **That is still broadly right and it is no longer uniformly right,
and the piece will look dated within a year if it does not say so.**

- **Delaware HB 191**, 23 April 2026: prohibits any nonhuman entity, including
  an AI-powered agent, from being licensed or certified to practise as a
  physician or physician assistant, and from using the protected titles.
- **Utah** is running a regulatory pilot that allows AI systems to autonomously
  renew certain routine prescriptions for patients with chronic conditions.
- **Idaho and Iowa** have introduced bills creating a licensed AI provider
  category, an "Artificial Intelligence Augmented and Autonomous Service
  Provider", with a Board of Autonomous Medical Practice, tiered licensing by
  degree of autonomy, and provisional sandbox licences.
- **Texas** has passed AI disclosure requirements for physicians.
- The **Federation of State Medical Boards** position is that AI is not ready to
  be independently licensed like a physician.

Sources: [Holland & Knight, states regulating AI in healthcare, May 2026](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/states-continue-efforts-to-regulate-ai-in-healthcare),
[STAT, FSMB leadership on licensing AI to practise medicine, 3 August 2026](https://www.statnews.com/2026/08/03/ai-doctors-licensing-state-medical-boards/),
[STAT on the Utah pilot, 11 May 2026](https://www.statnews.com/2026/05/11/ai-doctors-licenses-utah-doctronic-pilot/),
[TMLT on the Texas disclosure law](https://www.tmlt.org/resource/new-ai-disclosure-requirements-for-physicians-passed-into-texas-law).

**This is the best evidence in the note for his actual thesis.** Permission is
not a law of nature and it is not immovable. It is a defended preference,
currently being renegotiated state by state, at different speeds, with different
answers, inside a single country. Which is precisely what "the conditions are
national" predicts, one level down.

## 🔁 What this changes in the draft

1. **Rewrite the Spain row.** Free at the door, and the money returns at the
   pharmacy and the dentist. Availability is what binds: 102 days, four million
   people waiting.
2. **Keep the Belgium row, drop the implied ranking.** Visibility, not burden.
3. **Use the specialist figure for Australia**, not the GP gap. 92% pay.
4. **Use maternity care deserts for the US.** It is the county-level, physical,
   unfixable-by-price fact the argument needs.
5. **Add one clause to the permission row** noting Utah, Idaho and Iowa, so the
   piece reads as current rather than reflexively sceptical.

## ⚠️ Caveats and what I could not source

- **The OECD Health at a Glance 2025 chapter on financial hardship returned 403
  to me**, so the comparable four-country out-of-pocket table is assembled from
  national and secondary sources rather than one harmonised dataset. The
  Spain and Belgium shares are close enough (20.9% against 17-18%) that
  different methodologies could narrow or reverse the gap. **Do not put the two
  numbers side by side in the piece as a precise comparison**; use them
  qualitatively.
- The Cleanbill figures are a private survey of clinic-advertised pricing, not
  Medicare administrative data. AIHW is the authority where the two disagree.
- The Spanish waiting-list figures come via a news report of Ministry data
  rather than from the Ministry release directly. The Ministry portal is linked
  and should be checked before publication.
- **Nothing here is his opinion.** These are third-party facts that support,
  sharpen and in one case correct a table I wrote. Per `research/README.md`, if
  any of it changes what he thinks, that is a conversation and it lands in a
  capture.
