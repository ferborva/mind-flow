# ADKAR, and the last time we ran this experiment

Grounding for the transition piece. Two things: the change framework he named,
and the historical precedent that answers the question he is asking.

## 🦅 TL;DR

- **ADKAR is five sequential stages** and the whole point is that skipping one
  causes failure at the individual level. At population scale that becomes the
  central design problem: different cohorts sit at different stages
  simultaneously, and policy written for one damages another.
- **History already ran his transmission test, and the answer was no.** Engels'
  Pause, Britain 1780-1840: output per worker up 46%, real wages up 12%, profit
  rate doubled. **Fifty years of pause.**
- **Then it reversed hard.** 1840-1900: output up 90%, real wages up 123%. The
  catch-up was real. It just arrived a lifetime late.
- **What ended it is the actionable part.** Capital accumulation catching up
  with productivity, plus institutional reform: Factory Acts, the collapse of
  child labour, improved labour bargaining position.

## 🔄 ADKAR

Developed by Jeff Hiatt at Prosci in 2003, from change patterns observed across
more than 700 organisations. Five sequential building blocks:

| Stage | What it means | Failure mode if skipped |
|---|---|---|
| **A**wareness | Understanding *why* the change is needed | People experience change as something done to them |
| **D**esire | Personal motivation to participate | Compliance without commitment; quiet resistance |
| **K**nowledge | Knowing *how* to change | Willing people who cannot act |
| **A**bility | Actually applying it in practice | Training that never converts to behaviour |
| **R**einforcement | Making it stick | Reversion to the old state |

**The load-bearing principle:** organisational change only succeeds when each
affected *individual* makes the transition. If any single stage is incomplete
for a given person, the whole initiative hits a barrier at that person. Stages
must complete in order.

**Why that matters for his piece.** Applied to a civilisation rather than a
company, sequencing becomes the hard constraint. Most abundance advocacy is
broadcasting **Knowledge** ("here is how the future works") to a population that
has not been given **Awareness** ("this is happening to you specifically") and
has no **Desire** ("why would I want this"). That is a textbook ADKAR failure,
and it predicts exactly the resistance we observe.

## ⏳ Engels' Pause: the experiment already ran

Coined by economic historian Robert C. Allen, named for Engels' *The Condition
of the Working Class in England*.

**The pause, Britain roughly 1780-1840:**

| Measure | Change |
|---|---|
| Output per worker | **+46%** |
| Real wages | **+12%** |
| Profit rate | **Doubled** |
| Profit share of national income | Expanded, at the expense of labour and land |

**The catch-up, 1840-1900:**

| Measure | Change |
|---|---|
| Output per worker | **+90%** |
| Real wages | **+123%** |

**This is the transmission test, run at full scale, with an answer.** Prices did
not fall faster than wages disappeared. For fifty years the gains went to
capital, and the working population absorbed the difference. Then the
relationship inverted and wages more than doubled.

**Everything Fer is worried about happened, in order:**
- Productivity and wages decoupled.
- The profit share rose sharply and visibly.
- Concentration of returns to capital owners.
- Enormous social strain, and the political movements that came with it.

And everything the optimists promise also happened. Just fifty years later, to a
different generation.

## 🔧 What ended it

The most useful part, because it is the only bit that is actionable.

**Capital accumulation caught up with productivity growth.** Allen's central
claim. The capital stock eventually grew enough that labour regained scarcity
value. This is the mechanism that runs on its own, and it is slow.

**Institutional reform did the rest.** Factory Acts of the 1830s regulated hours
and child labour. Raising the cost of child labour had a second-order effect
worth noting: **it accelerated the adoption of steam power.** Regulation changed
the relative price of labour versus machine and moved investment. By the 1850s
child workforce participation had collapsed.

**Labour's bargaining position improved**, through both workforce composition
changes and organisation.

**The lesson for the piece.** The pause did not end because owners chose to
share. It ended because the capital stock saturated and because institutions
changed the price of the alternatives. That is a direct challenge to Fer's
original "the enterprises must choose" framing, and it supports his revised
one: **set the measures, change the relative prices, and the allocation
follows.**

## ⚠️ How far the analogy stretches

Worth stating in the piece, because a careful reader will push here.

**Where it holds.** A general purpose technology, rapid productivity growth, a
decoupling of output from wages, rising capital share, and a displaced
population with no immediate alternative.

**Where it may not.**
- Industrial machinery *complemented* labour, creating factory jobs even as it
  destroyed craft ones. Whether AI complements or substitutes at the same scale
  is the open question and the entire argument turns on it.
- The nineteenth century had somewhere for displaced agricultural labour to go.
  It is not obvious what the equivalent destination is.
- Global capital mobility, welfare states and universal franchise did not exist
  then. All three should compress the timeline.
- Fifty years then was roughly two generations. The same absolute duration now
  is a smaller share of a working life, but the technology is moving faster, so
  the *relative* shock may be larger.

**Net: use it as a warning and a template, not a forecast.** The honest claim is
"this exact pattern has happened before and took fifty years to resolve", not
"it will take fifty years again".
