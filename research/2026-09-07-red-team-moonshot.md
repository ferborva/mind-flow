# Red team: the abundance argument shredded from five directions

Counter-arguments built from the actual published positions of the people Fer
wants to persuade. **These are my constructions, not their words.** Nobody named
here has seen his piece. Each section is "the strongest objection this person's
own framework generates", which is a different and more useful thing than a
guess at what they would say.

## 🦅 TL;DR

- **Three of these land hard.** Wissner-Gross's, Diamandis's, and Mostaque's.
- **Two are survivable** but need a sentence of pre-emption in the piece.
- **The most dangerous one is not on this list.** It is the arithmetic in
  `2026-09-07-abundance-figures.md`: the profits he wants redistributed amount
  to eight cents per person per day. Fix that before anything else.
- The single best defensive move is to **stop arguing that companies should be
  generous and start arguing that they are the only actor with the right cost
  structure.** Same conclusion, immune to most of what follows.

## 🚀 Peter Diamandis: you are measuring the wrong variable

*Framework: the 6Ds. Digitised, deceptive, disruptive, dematerialised,
demonetised, democratised. Demonetisation is the mechanism, not a side effect.*

**The objection.** You have written two thousand words about how money gates
access, and you never once asked what the thing costs. That is the only variable
that matters, and it is the one collapsing.

Between 1969 and 1989 you would have paid roughly **$900,000** for the cameras,
video cameras, CD players, stereos, encyclopaedias, atlases, GPS units and
answering machines that now arrive free with a phone. The cost of food has
dropped thirteenfold in a century. Uber demonetised a trillion-dollar automotive
market before autonomy even arrived.

**You have built your entire argument on a static price list.** Every `if you can
afford it` in your table is a snapshot of a number in freefall. Ask not "who can
pay $10,950 a year for a decent life", ask "what does a decent life cost in
2040 when housing is printed, energy is solar-marginal-zero, diagnosis is free
and education is an agent". The conditional does not need to be redistributed
away. It gets deleted by the denominator going to zero.

**Where it lands.** Hard, and it is the objection his piece is least prepared
for. His Cabernet example is a genuine rebuttal for positional and
time-scarce goods, but he never generalises it into a reply on ordinary goods.

**The defence he has and does not use.** Demonetisation has not touched the
things that actually consume a household budget. Housing, healthcare and
education have gone the *other* way in every developed economy for forty years.
Baumol's cost disease says the labour-intensive services humans most want are
precisely the ones that do not demonetise. **The smartphone got free. The rent
did not.** That is his answer and it is a good one. It should be in the piece.

Sources: [Diamandis on demonetised cost of living](https://www.diamandis.com/blog/demonetized-cost-of-living),
[Singularity University on the 6Ds](https://www.su.org/resources/a-new-proposal-measuring-social-impact-in-an-exponential-world),
[Thought Economics interview](https://thoughteconomics.com/peter-diamandis/)

## 🧬 Alexander Wissner-Gross: you have described the objective function

*Framework: causal entropic forces. Intelligence is a physical force that
maximises future freedom of action, preserving as many future histories as
possible.*

**The objection.** Your `if` conditions are, formally, constraints on future
freedom of action. You have listed them: capital, stock, geography, supply chain,
legality, age, certification. Each one prunes the set of futures reachable from
here.

Now notice what you have done. **You have written a careful taxonomy of exactly
the quantity that intelligence, by definition, maximises.** If intelligence is
the force that preserves future histories, then a sufficiently capable
intelligence is a constraint-dissolving engine pointed directly at your list.
You have not identified a limit on the abundance thesis. You have specified its
objective function and handed it over.

Your national-boundaries argument is the weakest instance. Borders are
low-entropy arrangements maintained at enormous cost. They are exactly the sort
of structure that a system optimising for reachable futures routes around, and
the internet already demonstrated the routing.

**Where it lands.** Hard, and it is the most intellectually interesting attack.
It reframes his whole list as a to-do list for the technology rather than an
obstacle to it.

**The defence.** Two moves. First, "dissolvable in principle" and "dissolved on a
human timeline" are different claims, and he is making a claim about transition,
not about limits. Second, and better: **some of his conditions are not
constraints on the intelligence, they are constraints imposed by other humans
who benefit from them.** Legality, licensing and borders are not physics. They
are somebody's preference, defended. An optimiser does not dissolve a border; it
negotiates with the people holding it.

Sources: [Wissner-Gross & Freer, Causal Entropic Forces, Phys. Rev. Lett. 110, 168702](https://www.alexwg.org/publications/PhysRevLett_110-168702.pdf),
[APS Physics summary](https://physics.aps.org/articles/v6/46),
[AIP Inside Science](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/physicist-proposes-new-way-to-think-about-intelligence)

## ⚡ Emad Mostaque: right diagnosis, worst possible remedy

*Framework: Universal Basic AI. Compute and intelligent agency as inalienable
rights, delivered through collectively-owned infrastructure and Proof of
Beneficial Compute, not corporate goodwill.*

**The objection.** We agree on the diagnosis, and that should worry you, because
I have spent years on the remedy and yours is the one I rejected first.

**The concentration of cognitive power in a handful of US corporations is the
threat.** Not rogue AGI. You have seen it clearly. And then you conclude that the
solution is to ask those same corporations, nicely, to give things away.

You have identified a principal-agent problem and proposed that the agent fix it
voluntarily. A company that gives away its service at zero cost while its
competitor does not, loses. You said so yourself: you called it a deadlock, and
then you asked them to step out of it anyway, on ethics. That is not a
mechanism. **A mechanism is something that works when nobody involved is
virtuous.**

Your Tesla gyms and free clinics are structurally correct and institutionally
naive. The right version is the same service, delivered by infrastructure nobody
owns, funded by an incentive that pays for beneficial compute rather than
attention. You want the outcome of a protocol and the governance of a charity.

**Where it lands.** Hardest of all, because it comes from inside his own
conclusion. It concedes his framing entirely and destroys his last section.

**The defence.** Thin. The honest answer is that Mostaque is probably right and
the piece should say so. Fer's strongest available reply is that a protocol still
needs the compute, the models and the energy, all of which are currently owned
by the same handful of corporations, so the transition problem does not go away,
it relocates. That is a real point but it is a concession, not a rebuttal.

Sources: [Intelligent Internet whitepaper](https://ii.inc/whitepaper),
[Cognitive Revolution interview on UBAI](https://www.cognitiverevolution.ai/emad-mostaque-on-the-intelligent-internet-and-universal-basic-ai/),
[Aleare Research on II](https://alearesearch.substack.com/p/intelligent-internet-using-ai-x-crypto)

## 🏢 Salim Ismail: you are betting on companies that will not exist

*Framework: Exponential Organizations. Linear incumbents get obliterated by ExOs
achieving the same impact at a fraction of cost and time. Access triumphs over
ownership. Organisational immune systems reject the change that would save them.*

**The objection.** Your argument has a load-bearing assumption you never state:
that the companies accruing the profits today are the ones who will still be
accruing them when the choice has to be made.

Everything I have documented says otherwise. **Large matrixed organisations find
disruptive change extremely difficult, and their immune systems attack the
transformation as a threat.** The list of firms that dominated a decade ago and
are now case studies is long. You are asking the S&P 500 of 2026 to make a
multi-decade allocation decision, and the S&P 500 of 2046 is substantially
companies that do not exist yet.

The second problem is your framing of scarcity. **You are arguing about who owns
things in a world moving to access over ownership.** Nobody needs to own the
Cabernet. Your limited-atoms argument is a real constraint on ownership and
almost irrelevant to access, which is where value is going.

**Where it lands.** Glancing, but it exposes something real. His entire final
section is addressed to an audience he assumes is permanent.

**The defence.** Good, and he half has it already. He explicitly notes that a
company survives only "if there's nothing better in the market", and that when
that fails "the profits will move someplace else". **The concentration is
structural even when the occupants rotate.** He is not addressing Google, he is
addressing whoever is standing where Google is standing. He should make that
explicit, because right now it reads as a claim about named firms.

On access versus ownership: it works for reproducible goods and fails for
positional and time-scarce ones. The Cabernet was already his answer.

Sources: [Exponential Organizations summary](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-exponential-organizations/),
[OpenExO on ExOs](https://medium.com/openexo/forget-startups-exos-exponential-organizations-are-the-new-way-to-innovate-32305d628928),
[Salim Ismail](https://salimismail.com/)

## 💼 Dave Blundin: the reinvestment answer is not a dodge

*Framework: the venture capital seat. Compounding capital into productive
capacity, and the incentive structure that produces the thing you want to
distribute.*

**The objection.** You raise the reinvestment argument, call it a framing device,
and move on with a "for now" hanging off it. That is the weakest paragraph in the
piece, because reinvestment is not a public relations line. It is the actual
answer.

Capital allocated into compute, energy and infrastructure compounds into the
productive capacity that makes your decent life cheap. Capital distributed as
transfers is consumed once. **You are proposing to convert the fastest
compounding engine in economic history into a consumption subsidy, and calling
it the moral option.** Alphabet's capital expenditure guidance for 2026 alone is
$175bn to $185bn. That spending is the abundance you are asking to be given.

And your threshold question is worse than it looks. Cap accrual at $1.1m per
person and you have capped the upside that funds every high-variance bet in the
portfolio. The returns are power-law. The one in fifty that works pays for the
forty-nine that did not, and it does so because the winner is uncapped.

**Where it lands.** It bruises. His piece genuinely under-argues this, and the
"for now" is doing work it has not earned.

**The defence.** Strong, and it is the sharpest thing in his rant if he develops
it. **Compounding into productive capacity only reaches people through a wage or
a price.** If the labour channel is being deliberately automated away, one of the
two transmission mechanisms is being severed by the same investment. Reinvestment
is a complete answer only if prices fall faster than wages disappear, which is a
testable claim and nobody is testing it. That is his rebuttal, and it is good.

Source for capex: [Alphabet FY2025 results](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1652044/000165204426000012/googexhibit991q42025.htm)
($91.4bn in 2025, $175-185bn guided for 2026).

## 🧭 Verdict: what actually needs fixing

**Survives intact.** The `verb + blank + if` structure. Nobody above lays a
finger on it. It is a genuinely good observation and the data supports it harder
than the piece currently claims: four in five people live below $30 a day.

**Survives with repair.**
- *The national conditions argument* needs the Wissner-Gross defence built in:
  borders are not physics, they are defended preferences.
- *The "companies must choose" argument* needs Ismail's correction: address the
  position, not the incumbents.
- *The reinvestment paragraph* needs the Blundin answer met head on with the
  transmission-mechanism reply, which is his best unplayed card.

**Does not survive as written.**
- *Redistribution as the remedy.* Eight cents per person per day. This is not an
  objection from the podcast, it is arithmetic, and it is fatal to the current
  framing. He needs to arrive at free services because redistribution is
  impossible by a factor of twenty, not because companies ought to be generous.
- *Corporate discretion as the mechanism.* Mostaque has already refuted it and
  built the alternative. Concede it and engage with UBAI, or lose the section.

**The unplayed card.** Baumol. Diamandis's demonetisation is real for
manufactured and digital goods and has failed completely for housing, healthcare
and education, which are most of a household budget. **The smartphone got free
and the rent did not.** One paragraph on that turns his weakest flank into his
strongest, and it is the kind of first-principles observation the group would
actually enjoy being hit with.
