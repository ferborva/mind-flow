# Healthcare IF validation

> **Research note, not a cross-country ranking or policy recommendation.** This
> note checks the healthcare example in `drafts/name-the-if.md` against current
> primary government sources. It does not identify a binding condition for any
> individual, prove that AI diagnosis is effective, or show that one health
> system is better.

## 🦅 TL;DR

- The four-country example survives only as a demonstration that **price,
  permission, proximity, availability and capability can vary by person,
  service, place, provider and time**.
- It cannot honestly say that one condition binds for an entire country. The
  sources use different services, populations, dates and statistical units.
- The strongest current facts are narrower: Spain reports material specialist
  waits; Belgium uses partial reimbursement and can retain co-payments or
  supplements; Australia combines bulk-billed and out-of-pocket GP care with
  geographic workforce gaps; and the United States combines tens of millions
  of uninsured people with measured cost-related unmet need.
- “The technology is identical” is not defensible. Model access, clinical
  validation, integration, language, law, infrastructure and provider choices
  can also differ.

## 🔬 Method

Sources were limited to national government publishers or their official
statistical agencies. Each claim below records its population, statistical
unit, denominator or frame, reference period and permitted wording. Sources
were retrieved on 2026-09-09.

This is not a harmonised comparison. No common patient journey, service basket,
clinical need, severity, waiting-time definition or cost denominator was found
across all four countries. The evidence therefore supports examples of
conditions, not a league table or national “binding IF” verdict.

## 🇪🇸 Spain

The Spanish Ministry of Health states that the common basic care portfolio is
fully covered by public financing, while supplementary services such as
pharmaceuticals, orthoprosthetics, dietetic products and non-urgent transport
may require a user contribution. This supports “many basic services have no
charge at the point of care”, not “healthcare is free” without qualification.

At 31 December 2025, the national waiting-list report recorded about 4 million
people, or 84 per 1,000 population, waiting for a first hospital specialist
consultation. The patient-weighted mean wait was 102 days, and 61.5% of listed
patients had an appointment assigned more than 60 days away. The report warns
that the source data are supplied by autonomous communities and that a 2024
method change limits longer trend comparison.

**Permitted draft wording:** Spain's publicly financed basic portfolio reduces
many point-of-care price barriers, while the December 2025 specialist register
shows that availability and waiting time can still constrain access.

Sources:

- [Spanish Ministry of Health, common service portfolio](https://www.sanidad.gob.es/profesionales/prestacionesSanitarias/CarteraDeServicios/)
- [Spanish Ministry of Health, waiting-list report at 31 December 2025](https://www.sanidad.gob.es/estadEstudios/estadisticas/inforRecopilaciones/docs/Informe_situacion_listas_de_espera_dic_2025_V1.pdf)

## 🇧🇪 Belgium

Belgium's Federal Public Service for Health says patients receive an invoice,
and people affiliated with the compulsory system can often receive full or
partial reimbursement for recognised services. Contracted providers follow
negotiated tariffs and patients pay the co-payment. Non-contracted providers
may add supplements. This source explains mechanics, not the distribution of
actual household burden or waiting time.

**Permitted draft wording:** Belgium makes prices visible and often reimburses
part of recognised care, but co-payments, provider status, supplements and
eligibility mean price is not “cancelled”.

Source:

- [Belgian FPS Health, invoices and prices in the healthcare system](https://www.health.belgium.be/en/themes/health/je-gezondheid/grensoverschrijdende-gezondheidszorg/zorg-het-buitenland/invoices-prices-belgian-healthcare-system)

## 🇦🇺 Australia

The Australian Department of Health says Medicare provides free or subsidised
care. A bulk-billed service has no patient charge, while a patient whose doctor
does not bulk bill pays the difference between the doctor's charge and the
Medicare benefit. The national snapshot reports that 81.4% of GP non-referred
attendances from 1 November 2025 to 31 January 2026 were bulk billed. The unit
is a service, not a unique patient or a guarantee that any named person can get
an appointment.

The department also says about 7 million people, 29% of the population in its
cited frame, live in regional and rural Australia and can face long waits or
distances to care. Geographic classifications identify areas with GP shortages.

**Permitted draft wording:** Australia mixes no-charge and out-of-pocket GP
services. Price depends on billing and eligibility, while proximity and
availability differ materially by location.

Sources:

- [Australian Department of Health, Medicare costs](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/medicare/about/costs)
- [Australian Department of Health, national GP bulk-billing snapshot](https://www.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-02/national-gp-bulk-billing-snapshot-1-november-2025-to-31-january-2026.pdf)
- [Australian Department of Health, doctors and specialists outside metropolitan areas](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/doctors-and-specialists/what-we-do)
- [Australian Department of Health, Distribution Priority Areas](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/rural-health-workforce/classifications/dpa)

## 🇺🇸 United States

The US Census Bureau estimated that 27.1 million people, 8.0% of the population,
had no health insurance at any point during 2024. The coverage measure does not
show whether an insured person could afford a particular service.

The National Center for Health Statistics reports that 6.0% of adults aged 18
or older failed to obtain needed medical care because of cost in the 2025
National Health Interview Survey early release. CMS reports $556.6 billion of
out-of-pocket health spending in 2024, including direct payments, deductibles,
co-payments and coinsurance under its accounting definition. HRSA reports that
35% of US counties lacked both an obstetric facility and obstetric providers in
the cited maternity-care source. These are different estimands and must not be
combined into one rate.

**Permitted draft wording:** In the United States, price and coverage remain
material access conditions for millions, while proximity and provider
availability also fail for particular services and places.

Sources:

- [US Census Bureau, Health Insurance Coverage in the United States: 2024](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2025/demo/p60-288.html)
- [CDC/NCHS, Access to Health Care](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/fastats/access-to-health-care.htm)
- [CMS, National Health Expenditure fact sheet](https://www.cms.gov/data-research/statistics-trends-and-reports/national-health-expenditure-data/nhe-fact-sheet)
- [HRSA, maternal health access facts](https://www.hrsa.gov/maternal-health)

## 📏 Source-by-source claim ceiling

| Claim | Ceiling | What the source does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Spanish basic care is publicly financed | System rule for the common basic portfolio | Zero cost for every service or person |
| Spain had a 102-day mean first-specialist wait | Registered patients, first hospital specialist consultations, 31 December 2025 | An individual's wait, care quality or unmet clinical need |
| Belgium commonly reimburses recognised care | Financing mechanism for eligible care | Near-zero burden, universal eligibility or timely access |
| Australia recorded 81.4% bulk billing | GP non-referred attendance services, Nov 2025 to Jan 2026 | Share of people with free access or appointment availability |
| 27.1 million US residents were uninsured | Persons without coverage at any point in 2024 under CPS ASEC | Cost burden among insured people or inability to obtain care |
| 6.0% of US adults missed needed care due to cost | Adults in the 2025 NHIS early release | Which treatment, outcome or policy caused the result |

**None of these observations establishes the binding condition for a country,
let alone for a named person.** A binding-IF claim needs the person's complete
care journey, viable alternative routes, clinical need, eligibility, price,
travel, wait, provider capacity, permission and capability in the same scope.

## 🛑 Claims withdrawn or narrowed

| Earlier wording | Disposition | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| “Diagnosis genuinely goes towards zero” | Withdraw | No registered full-cost estimand, clinical validation or delivery scope |
| “The model cannot prescribe, refer or order a scan in almost any jurisdiction” | Withdraw | Sweeping legal claim with no jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction review |
| “The condition that binds is not the one anyone is working on” | Narrow | Requires evidence about both binding conditions and active work |
| “Price is nearly cancelled” in Belgium | Narrow | Partial reimbursement can retain co-payments and supplements |
| “The technology in all four countries is identical” | Withdraw | Deployment, validation, integration, language and regulation differ |

## 🔁 What this validates

The healthcare example supports the **method hypothesis**: a universal promise
becomes more useful when rewritten for a named person, service, standard, place,
period and explicit IF set. It also demonstrates why conditions should not be
ranked once for an entire country.

It does not validate the abundance thesis, AI diagnosis, the five-prompt set as
complete, or the Observatory as an effective intervention. Those remain open
claims with separate evidence needs.
